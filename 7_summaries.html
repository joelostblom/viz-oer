7  Summaries

NoteLearning outcomes & Chapter summary

  1. Visualize measures of central tendency, such as means and medians, using statistical summaries.

  2. Show variability with standard deviations, percentile intervals, and boxplots.

  3. Explain what summaries can hide and combine them with the underlying observations using jittered points.

7.1 Why bother?

In Order, we connected observations to make their sequence clear. Sometimes, however, our question concerns a collection of observations: How many are there? What is their total? What is a typical value? A summary helps answer these questions by reducing many values to a few. In this chapter, we will explore how to reduce a collection of observations to useful quantities.

That reduction is useful, but it also leaves information out. As we work through this chapter, keep asking: What did we lose, and how much of it do we need to put back? We will explore these questions here and in the following chapters.

Ex-promptExercise 1

Suppose you are interested in whether meditation could help you feel less stressed before your next exam. The dataset below is a 500-observation sample from a randomized study by Sparacio and colleagues.1 It contains stress scores from four meditation conditions and a control condition in which participants listened to a story excerpt. The score averages 20 items from the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory’s state-anxiety questionnaire (STAI Form Y-1), after reverse-scoring the relevant items; it ranges from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater self-reported distress after the activity. How do the recorded stress scores differ between conditions? We start by applying the strategies we have learned so far: grouping observations by position and comparing their values on a shared scale.

  • Altair
  • ggplot
import pandas as pd
import altair as alt

meditation = pd.read_csv('data/meditation-stress-sample.csv')

alt.Chart(meditation).mark_point().encode(
    y='condition',
    x='stress'
)
library(readr)
library(ggplot2)

meditation <- read_csv('data/meditation-stress-sample.csv')
ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    geom_point()

What conclusions can you draw?

Ex-hintHint

Can you tell where most observations lie in each condition? Could several observations share the same position in the chart?

Ex-solutionSolution

Although we followed the strategies we have learned so far, overlapping points make it difficult to compare where observations are concentrated. We can see the range of recorded scores, but not how many observations share each position. Read on to find out how we could make these comparisons easier.

7.2 Visualizing statistical summaries

In Why visualize data?, we saw that statistical summaries can make some patterns easier to identify than they are in a raw table of numbers. We can use a similar strategy here by comparing the mean stress score in each condition.

  • Altair
  • ggplot

In Altair, we can write a supported aggregation function such as mean, median, or sum directly as a string wrapping the column name, for example, 'mean(stress)':

alt.Chart(meditation).mark_point().encode(
    y='condition',
    x='mean(stress)'
)

In ggplot, we can tell geom_point() to use a summary statistic instead of the individual observation values. We set stat = 'summary' and specify a function that returns a single value, such as mean, median, or sum. For geom_point(), the default stat is 'identity', which uses the supplied values directly; other geoms can have different defaults.

ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    geom_point(stat = 'summary', fun = mean)

Instead of calling geom_point() and setting the stat parameter, we could call stat_summary() and set the geom parameter:

ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    stat_summary(geom = 'point', fun = mean)

These two calls produce the same chart, and both are commonly used. Sticking with the geom_ approach makes the code syntax more consistent, whether we are plotting individual observations or summaries. This Stack Overflow answer explains the relationship between geoms and stats in more detail.



Should the axis include zero?

As you can see, different packages make different decisions about whether to include zero by default. For point summaries, the appropriate axis range depends on the comparisons we want to make and the meaning of the scale. We will discuss axis limits in more detail later.

Now we can clearly see that on average there are some differences between the groups.

7.3 Visualizing spread

But showing only the mean hides the variability in the observations. How the individual values are distributed also affects the conclusions we can draw from the data.

So, whenever we show a measure of central tendency such as the mean or median, it is helpful to pair it with an indication of the underlying variability.

Here, we need to distinguish two concepts: the spread of the underlying data (descriptive statistics) and the uncertainty in the estimate of central tendency (inferential statistics). These answer two different questions, but graphically they are often depicted similarly. In this chapter, we will focus on how spread out the underlying data distribution is, and return to confidence intervals and other measures of inferential uncertainty in a future chapter.

One measure of the spread in the underlying data is the standard deviation, which measures how spread out observations are around the mean.2 Let’s see how we can include the standard deviation together with the mean in our charts:

  • Altair
  • ggplot

The errorbar mark allows us to plot several common measures of variability, including an interval from one standard deviation below the mean to one above it. We’re changing the color of the point marks to match the error bar. We are using mark_circle as a shortcut for mark_point(filled=True). The point layer calculates the mean, while the error-bar layer calculates its interval from the individual stress scores.

base = alt.Chart(meditation).encode(
    y='condition',
    x='stress'
)
points = base.mark_circle(color='black').encode(x='mean(stress)')

points + base.mark_errorbar(extent='stdev', color='black')

As the name implies, the pointrange geom creates both a point and a range. Previously, we passed a summary function to the fun parameter. Here, we use fun.data because we need a central value and the lower and upper endpoints of the interval. Whereas fun accepts functions that return a single value, such as mean, fun.data accepts functions that return a dataframe with the summary and interval endpoints. In this horizontal chart, those values determine positions along the x-axis.

The fun.args parameter looks a bit cryptic. What it does is to pass mult = 1 (multiplier) to the mean_sdl function, which otherwise multiplies the standard deviation by 2 by default.

ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    geom_pointrange(stat = 'summary', fun.data = mean_sdl, fun.args = list(mult = 1))

The mean_sdl() function requires the Hmisc package. If it is missing, ggplot may report a failed statistical computation and omit the layer; check the rendering messages if the summary does not appear.

This gives us some additional information: we can see that all the conditions had a similar spread in the underlying data. While pairing a mean with an indication of its standard deviation is better than just showing a mean alone, it can still hide important features of the data. As we learned in the first chapter, statistical summaries don’t tell the whole story, even when we plot them. Let’s explore that more in this exercise.

Ex-promptExercise 2

To the left, we can see the mean ± one standard deviation for two groups of observations. To the right, there are four different suggestions for what these underlying observations might look like. By studying this chart, which sets of possible underlying observations do you think could produce the summary chart on the left?

Ex-hintHint

It’s more than just one of them…

Ex-solutionSolution

In this case, all four scenarios produce the same means and standard deviations for groups A and B! If you only look at the mean ± SD chart, you cannot tell which of these very different datasets it represents. This matters because depending on what your observations look like, you will come to different conclusions. For example, an outlier might be a genuine observation or a measurement error. Seeing it lets us investigate its origin and its influence on the summary. Or what if group A follows a symmetric distribution whereas group B shows a bimodal spread?

Note that in the case of the bimodal distribution of observations we should ask ourselves whether it is at all useful to include a mean. Maybe we should omit it here, since few of the observations are actually close to the mean and the mean therefore does not tell us much about the underlying observations. In this case, it may be more helpful to talk about the two clusters instead of trying to represent all the observations with a single average value.

7.4 Including the underlying observations

To avoid being misled by a lone summary statistic, we can show the underlying observations of each group together with the statistical summaries. Plotting every observation in a group along the same line can lead to overlap. Jittering adds small random offsets to the points to help separate them. Here, we jitter only along the categorical y-axis, so each point’s horizontal position still represents its recorded stress score.

Jitterbug

Back in the 1930s, the jitterbug was a lively, often wild, dance style that matched the fast-paced, unpredictable energy of swing music. In data visualization, jittering serves a similar purpose: to add a touch of randomness to points in a plot.

  • Altair
  • ggplot

We calculate a random value for each observation and use yOffset to spread the points within each condition’s row. We then layer the mean and standard deviation interval over those points.

base = alt.Chart(meditation, height=alt.Step(30)).encode(
    y=alt.Y('condition').scale(padding=0.4),
    x='stress'
)

points = (
    base
    .transform_calculate(jitter=alt.expr.random())
    .mark_circle(color='lightgrey')
    .encode(
        yOffset=alt.YOffset('jitter:Q'),
        x='stress'
    )
)

points + base.mark_errorbar(extent='stdev', thickness=1.5) + base.mark_circle(size=40, color='black').encode(x='mean(stress)')
ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    geom_point(position = 'jitter', color = 'grey') +
    geom_pointrange(stat = 'summary', fun.data = mean_sdl, fun.args = list(mult = 1))

As a shortcut for jittered points you could also use the specialized geom geom_jitter().

7.5 Showing medians with boxplots

What if we visualize the median instead of the mean? Showing the median can be especially useful when there are a few extreme values in the data since these impact the mean more than the median, and the median thus becomes a more helpful indication of a typical value. But this decision is context-dependent: if we are interested in these extreme values, the mean may capture their influence in a way that is relevant to our question. Therefore, it is often a good idea to show means and medians together, since they tell us different things about the data.

A common measure of spread used together with medians is a percentile interval, which marks the range between two chosen percentiles. For comparison, the interval from one standard deviation below the mean to one above it contains about 68% of a normal distribution.

A common way to show the median and a percentile interval is a boxplot. The box spans the 25th to the 75th percentile, enclosing the middle 50% of the data, with a line marking the median. In the default boxplots below, the whiskers extend to the most extreme observations within 1.5 interquartile ranges of the box edges; observations beyond the whiskers are drawn separately. This illustration can help you interpret the different parts of the box:

  • Altair
  • ggplot
alt.Chart(meditation).mark_boxplot().encode(
    y='condition',
    x='stress'
)
ggplot(meditation, aes(y = condition, x = stress)) +
    geom_boxplot()

Although boxplots show multiple percentiles, they still hide important information about the underlying observations and should therefore also ideally be used together with a plot of the underlying observations or as a concise summary of the data after having inspected the underlying observations ourselves.

WarningAvoid drawing conclusions from summary charts alone

Summary charts such as mean ± SD plots and boxplots are useful since aggregation reduces the complexity of the chart. But even if you are interested in comparing group summaries such as means or medians, you should always also inspect the underlying observations to understand how they are distributed and whether there are any outliers, since this affects the conclusions you can draw from the chart.

7.6 What conclusions can we draw from the data?

The charts show differences in recorded stress scores between conditions in this sample. However, we have not assessed the statistical significance of those differences. Standard deviations and boxplots describe the spread of the observations, not the uncertainty in the group means or their differences. In particular, overlap between these descriptive intervals does not establish whether a difference is statistically significant. That would require an appropriate inferential analysis, informed by the study design.

What about the magnitude of the differences? In this dataset, the meditation conditions have mean stress scores about 0.14–0.28 units below the story-listening control condition. Is that a lot or a little? These are differences on the 1–4 questionnaire scale described above; judging their practical importance also requires considering what difference would matter in this context. The charts help us describe the sample, but they do not by themselves establish that meditation caused the differences or that the differences are large enough to matter in practice.

The original study’s main analysis, using 2,239 participants across 37 sites, found evidence that all four 15-minute mindfulness exercises reduced self-reported stress relative to the active control. The largest mean difference was for body scan: 0.27 points lower than the control on the 1–4 scale. The authors concluded that even these brief exercises can offer modest, short-term benefits.
