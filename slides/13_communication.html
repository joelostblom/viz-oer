Communication

Learning outcomes

  1. Design a visualization around the message and audience, using guidelines as a starting point rather than rigid rules.
  2. Format axis and legend values, titles, and ranges to make a figure understandable without a spoken explanation.
  3. Distinguish filtering observations from zooming into a chart, since filtering can change statistical summaries.

[During data exploration, you are often the main person interpreting a chart.]{slide-bullet} For communication, a figure needs to make sense to someone who does not already know the data or the plotting code. That reader might encounter it without the surrounding text or a spoken explanation. Readable labels, an informative title, and an appropriate view of the data are therefore part of the explanation itself.

Many useful design recommendations are collected in the Cato Institute’s visualization style guide. Other collections include these style guidelines, this spreadsheet of visualization guides, and the Data Visualization Society’s resources. Another way to learn is from other people’s mistakes: the Economist’s review of its own charts contains useful examples.

These are guidelines, not rules that work independently of the audience and context. For example, a familiar association with a fruit or political party may matter more than using the default palette. This discussion of breaking visualization rules illustrates how a deliberate exception can sometimes make a visualization more memorable. The important question is whether the choice helps readers understand the data.

The examples below use (mostly) published figures to show how design choices affect what a reader notices and concludes. Treat them as prompts to ask what comparison the reader needs to make.

Design shapes the message

Titles, colors, and graphical direction can emphasize different aspects of a dataset. Ask what message each design brings forward, and what important context it leaves behind. Match the detail to the audience

Match the detail to the audience

The simplified chart emphasizes the scale of the losses but discards route, timing, and conditions. Even a concise label needs checking: a reduction in army strength does not, by itself, establish how many soldiers died.

Make category comparisons easy

Positions and lengths on a common scale are usually easier to compare than angles and areas. A bar chart can also place each category name beside the value it identifies.

Use color to add information

When a chart shows one series, extra colors can imply distinctions that do not exist. A single color often provides a clear starting point.

Use color to add information

Use a contrasting color to draw attention to the observation or series that matters for the current question. Keep the remaining observations as context.

Make long labels readable

Change the chart orientation when that gives category names room to be read naturally. Horizontal bars often preserve the comparison while avoiding rotated or heavily wrapped text.

Ask what a chart does not show

An increasing number of horror releases does not establish that horror’s share of all films is increasing. Ask what denominator would be needed to support that claim.

Ask what a chart does not show

Adding total releases changes the question we can answer. To discuss relative popularity, examine the horror share alongside counts, and consider whether dataset coverage or reporting has changed.

Ask what a chart does not show

A cumulative total of nonnegative sales cannot decrease. Its increasing height alone does not show that the current rate of sales is accelerating.

Ask what a chart does not show

Quarterly sales correspond to increments in the cumulative total, not its height. Showing both can reveal changes in the rate that an ever-increasing total conceals.

Avoid redundant 3D

Perspective and occlusion make both heights and colored segments harder to compare. Use a third spatial dimension when it represents something the reader needs to understand, rather than simply as decoration.

Avoid redundant 3D

Textures, gradients, tilted axes, and competing colors can obscure the values. Readers should be able to answer the data question without first decoding the decoration.
