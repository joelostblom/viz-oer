Trends

Learning outcomes

  1. Use means and rolling means to summarize patterns while retaining the observations as context.
  2. Distinguish global regression models from local smoothing, and choose a trendline according to the question and audience.
  3. Adjust smoothing neighborhoods and explain how they affect the local detail shown by a curve.
  4. Recognize that describing a trend within the observed data does not justify extrapolating it beyond that range.

A trendline can make a general pattern easier to see when individual observations or multiple groups obscure it. Trendlines are also called fitted lines or lines of best fit. The choice of line determines which features of the data are emphasized. Here we focus on the summaries themselves and keep the observations visible. In Uncertainty, we add intervals and distinguish the spread of observations from uncertainty in an estimated mean.

We use automobile specifications from the Vega datasets collection, with missing acceleration or horsepower observations omitted. Acceleration records the time in seconds to accelerate from 0 to 60 mph: a larger value means the car takes longer to reach that speed. We examine how horsepower varies with this acceleration time, separately by origin.

Rolling means

  • Binning uses fixed, non-overlapping neighborhoods.

  • Overlapping neighborhoods that move along the axis produces a smoother curve

    • Referred to as a moving or rolling mean

  • Each car included in a window receives equal weight by default, but this could be adjusted

Binning uses fixed, non-overlapping neighborhoods. If we instead use, overlapping neighborhoods that move along the axis produces a smoother curve; this is referred to as a moving or rolling mean. Here we sort the individual cars by acceleration time and average neighboring observations, separately for each origin. Each car included in a window receives equal weight by default, but this could be adjusted

Rolling means

alt.Chart(cars).transform_window(
    rolling_hp='mean(Horsepower)',
    frame=[-30, 30],
    sort=[alt.SortField('Acceleration')],
    groupby=['Origin']

    x=alt.X('Acceleration:Q'),

    color='Origin'

rolling_cars <- cars |>
    arrange(Origin, Acceleration) |>
    mutate(
        rolling_hp = slider::slide_dbl(
            Horsepower,
            mean,
            .before = 30,
            .after = 30
        ),
        .by = Origin
    )
ggplot(rolling_cars, aes(x = Acceleration, y = rolling_hp, color = Origin)) +
    geom_line()

Altair

frame=[-30, 30] starts with the preceding 30 observations, the current observation, and the following 30 observations in acceleration-time order, within each origin. At the endpoints, it uses the available observations. By default, Altair expands window boundaries to include ties in the sorting variable, so a window can include more than 61 cars when acceleration times repeat.

ggplot

There is no built-in way to plot a rolling mean in ggplot, so instead we calculate the means before plotting. For each row, we find the acceleration times at the window’s endpoints and include all cars between those values, including boundary ties as in Altair.

Choosing a rolling window

  • Expanding ([None, 0], the default in Altair): average everything up to the current position in the sort order. This estimates a cumulative level rather than a local trend.
  • Trailing (for example, [-30, 0]): use observations at or before the current position. Common in e.g. finance. With a chronological sort, this is useful for monitoring as observations arrive.
  • Centered ([-30, 30]): use observations on both sides to describe a local pattern. Common in e.g. statistics.

The width of 30 observations on either side is an illustrative starting point, not a generally optimal choice. Compare narrower and wider windows to see which features persist. A fixed observation count spans different ranges of acceleration times where cars are more or less densely represented, and represents different fractions of origins with different sample sizes. Repeated acceleration times can also receive different estimates as the row window moves, producing vertical segments when those estimates are connected at the same x-value.

Regression lines

  • Linear least-squares regression fits a straight line by minimizing the sum of squared vertical deviations from the observations to the line.

  • The mean is the constant that minimizes the sum of squared deviations

    • The best-fitting horizontal line under squared-error loss

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 = \bar{y} \]

  • Linear regression allows that line to tilt by estimating a slope in addition to the intercept

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 + x_{i1}w_1 \]

  • Where \(i\) indexes cars, \(x_{i1}\) is car \(i\)’s acceleration time in seconds, and \(\hat{y}_i\) is its predicted mean horsepower.

Another way to show a trend is regression. Unlike separate means at each acceleration time, a regression links estimates across acceleration times through shared coefficients. Changing observations in one part of the x-range can change fitted values throughout the range. This pooling can stabilize estimates, but it can also hide abrupt changes or impose an unsuitable shape; a shared model is not automatically better.

Linear least-squares regression fits a straight line by minimizing the sum of squared vertical deviations from the observations to the line. There is a useful connection to the mean: the mean is the constant that minimizes the sum of squared deviations. In graphical terms, it is the best-fitting horizontal line under squared-error loss. As an equation, we’re predicting (or estimating) the value of each y value using a constant offset (which is the mean of all y-values).

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 = \bar{y} \]

Linear regression allows that line to tilt by estimating a slope in addition to the intercept. For the straight-line fit, we estimate

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 + x_{i1}w_1 \]

where \(i\) indexes cars, \(x_{i1}\) is car \(i\)’s acceleration time in seconds, and \(\hat{y}_i\) is its predicted mean horsepower. The fitted weights \(w_0\) and \(w_1\) are the intercept and slope. The first subscript in \(x_{i1}\) identifies the observation; the second identifies the feature.

A quadratic model

  • For the degree-two polynomial below, we add a feature \(x_{i2} = x_{i1}^2\):

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 + x_{i1}w_1 + x_{i2}w_2 \]

  • The quadratic fit is still a linear model in its weights \(w_0\), \(w_1\), and \(w_2\), even though its graph can be curved in the original feature space.

Regression is not restricted to straight lines. For the degree-two polynomial below, we add a feature \(x_{i2} = x_{i1}^2\):

\[ \hat{y}_i = w_0 + x_{i1}w_1 + x_{i2}w_2 \]

The weight \(w_2\) on squared acceleration time allows the relationship to curve rather than have a constant slope. Both examples estimate a separate set of coefficients for each origin by minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals. The quadratic fit is still a linear model in its weights \(w_0\), \(w_1\), and \(w_2\), even though its graph can be curved in the original feature space.

Local smoothing

  • Local regression fits nearby observations in overlapping neighborhoods, allowing the trend to change across the x-axis.

  • We can think of a rolling mean as local constant regression: at each target x-value, fit a horizontal line to nearby observations and retain its height as the estimate.

  • Local linear regression instead fits a line with a slope, then evaluates that line at the target x-value.

Sometimes one equation is a poor description of the whole dataset. Local regression fits nearby observations in overlapping neighborhoods, allowing the trend to change across the x-axis. This is usually called LOESS (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) or LOWESS (locally weighted scatterplot smoothing). We can think of a rolling mean as local constant regression: at each target x-value, fit a horizontal line to nearby observations and retain its height as the estimate. Local linear regression instead fits a line with a slope, then evaluates that line at the target x-value. Moving the neighborhood and repeating these fits produces the curve. The animation builds up the red fitted curve from local fits.

Altair

Altair fits linear regression locally in each neighborhood in transform_loess.

ggplot

For computational efficiency, geom_smooth() automatically changes the method based on the number of data points. To ensure that we use LOESS, we therefore set it explicitly although it is the default for datasets up to 1000 observations per group. By default, ggplot fits quadratic regressions locally for each neighborhood.

Near the left edge of an increasing trend, most available neighbors lie to the right. A rolling mean averages their higher responses and can overestimate the response at the target. A local linear fit can account for the slope and evaluate the line at the target instead For observations lying exactly on a straight line, it recovers that line wherever the neighborhood contains enough distinct, positively weighted x-values. A local mean can also reproduce a straight trend in a symmetric neighborhood, but generally not at the boundaries or when the x-values are unevenly spaced.

In our examples, the rolling mean and regression both use individual cars as observations, so densely represented acceleration ranges contribute more observations to their fits. The rolling mean uses a centered observation window, expanded for boundary ties, with equal weight per car; LOESS instead selects a fraction of nearby cars and weights them by distance. To isolate the effect of a local constant versus a local slope, we would need to use the same input observations, neighborhoods, and weights.

When to choose which trendline?

  • Means and rolling means are useful when the summarized values should have a straightforward interpretation. It is poorly suited to extrapolation beyond the data range but can be particularly accessible to a general audience.
  • Regression models are useful when a simple equation is a reasonable description of the relationship and it is advantageous to describe the relationship with a known equation. Predictions beyond the observed range are generally more accurate than for models fitted using a local neighborhood, but they still rely on that the fitted equation hold beyong the observed range, and become more uncertain as they move further from the data.
  • LOESS is useful for describing patterns within the observed data without choosing one global equation. It often resembles the trend a reader would sketch by eye, and it generally usable for communication purposes although they are not as straightforward to understand as a rolling mean. It is poorly suited to extrapolation beyond the data range.
