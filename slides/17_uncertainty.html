Uncertainty

Learning outcomes

  1. Distinguish variability among observations from uncertainty in an estimated mean.
  2. Create and interpret confidence intervals displayed as bands and point ranges.
  3. Combine estimates and their intervals with the underlying observations or distributions.
  4. Communicate probabilities using frequency framing and hypothetical outcomes.
  5. Recognize misleading interpretations of error bars, shaded bands, and forecast cones.
  6. Explain how bootstrap resamples produce a percentile confidence interval for a mean.

Why bother?

  • A point or a line can look precise even when it represents an uncertain estimate.

  • An interval that is familiar to a statistician may convey something quite different to a general audience

A point or a line can look precise even when it represents an uncertain estimate. Showing uncertainty helps readers judge which conclusions the data support, but the visual representation also matters: an interval that is familiar to a statistician may convey something quite different to a general audience. We will first explore how to communicate uncertainty, then create interval plots in Altair and ggplot.

Framing probabilities as frequencies

When called upon to judge probability, people actually judge something else and believe they have judged probability – Daniel Kahneman

The lecture’s historical example compares three forecasts of Donald Trump’s chance of winning the 2016 US presidential election. These percentages describe the probability of winning, not the predicted share of votes.

Hint

An outcome with a probability below 50% is less likely than its alternative, but is it impossible? Think about rolling a particular number on a fair six-sided die.

Solution

No. A probabilistic forecast is not a promise that the most likely outcome will occur. Rolling a six does not establish that a die is unfair merely because that outcome had only a one-in-six chance. The models assigned different probabilities, so they did not express the same degree of uncertainty. A scoring rule can reward a forecast that assigned more probability to the observed outcome, but one election cannot establish a model’s long-run calibration.

We can make the probabilities easier to imagine by expressing them as frequencies: roughly 28, 15, or 2 wins out of 100 comparable hypothetical elections under each forecast. These are model-implied scenarios, not a claim that we observed 100 elections.

Dark seats represent scenarios in which Trump wins. Even in the first forecast, a dark seat is less likely than a light one, but selecting one would hardly be extraordinary.

The format can show more than a binary win or loss. FiveThirtyEight’s 2020 forecast displayed both a distribution of simulated electoral-vote outcomes and a sample of 100 scenarios. The scenario display combines a frequency statement with the possible margin of victory.

Solution

We need to know what the point estimates and intervals represent: for example, means with standard deviations, confidence intervals for means, or some other summary. For confidence intervals, we also need the confidence level and an explanation of how they were calculated. The same point-and-line marks can display all these quantities, so we cannot infer the interval definition from their shape.

In Central tendency & spread, we combined measures of central tendency with indications of how the observations vary. But the spread of the observations and the uncertainty in an estimated mean answer different questions. In Trends, we used lines to summarize horsepower without displaying intervals. Here we group the cars by model year and origin, then add intervals to the annual means. For example, cars can have very different horsepower values even when we have enough observations to estimate their mean relatively precisely.

Spread and uncertainty answer different questions

Display What it describes
Mean ± one standard deviation (SD) Spread of the individual observations around their mean
Mean ± one standard error (SE) Uncertainty in the estimated mean; approximately a 68% CI under a normal approximation
95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean An interval targeting 95% coverage; often approximately mean ± 1.96 SE, or calculated using another interval method

Same observations, different intervals

A shorter bar does not by itself mean that the data or model are better. Changing from SD to SE changes the question being answered; changing the confidence level changes the interval’s intended coverage. Choose the quantity that addresses the question and label it explicitly.

Interpreting confidence intervals

  • If we repeatedly sampled data and constructed intervals in the same way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the population quantity, provided the method’s assumptions hold

  • It is not an interval containing 95% of the individual observations.

  • Bootstrapping resamples observations with replacement from the observed data, calculates the mean for each resample, and uses the resulting distribution of means to construct an interval.

A confidence interval describes uncertainty in an estimate. For a 95% confidence interval, the intended interpretation is that if we repeatedly sampled data and constructed intervals in the same way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the population quantity, provided the method’s assumptions hold. It is not an interval containing 95% of the individual observations.

For the intervals around separately estimated means below, we will use bootstrap confidence intervals for the mean. Bootstrapping resamples observations with replacement from the observed data, calculates the mean for each resample, and uses the resulting distribution of means to construct an interval. The interactive example below shows the resampling process; later, we will use plotting functions that calculate these intervals for us.

The standard error describes uncertainty in the estimated mean. When its sampling distribution is approximately normal—as is often the case for sufficiently large samples—mean ± one SE is approximately a 68% confidence interval, and mean ± two SE is approximately a 95% confidence interval. Bootstrapping estimates this uncertainty, but does not guarantee a normal sampling distribution. Increasing the number of bootstrap resamples reduces simulation noise; it does not increase the original sample size or remove skewness in the sampling distribution.

Suppose we have observed the horsepower of 20 cars and want to estimate the population mean. We cannot repeatedly collect new samples from that population with the data already in hand. Instead, the bootstrap treats the observed sample as an empirical distribution from which we can draw new samples. For this illustration, we first restrict the dataset to cars with at most 120 horsepower, then reproducibly select 20 cars. This keeps the display compact; the target is the mean for this restricted group, not the mean for all cars.

  1. Resample with replacement: draw 20 observations from the observed sample. A car can appear more than once, and others may not appear at all.
  2. Calculate the resample’s mean: each resample contributes one value to the bootstrap distribution of means.
  3. Repeat and take percentiles: the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap means form a 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval for the population mean.

The three panels share one horsepower scale. Small dots in the first two panels show observations; repeated horsepower values stack vertically. The larger dot and orange horizontal interval at the top of the observed-sample panel summarize its mean and bootstrap confidence interval. The solid black line marks the current resample’s mean in its panel and in the histogram; it changes as you select different resamples. Build a confidence interval by bootstrapping

Show 95% percentile interval is enabled by default and can be switched off. The orange region and dashed boundaries mark the middle 95% of the bootstrap means currently included in the histogram. The endpoints are calculated from the means themselves, not from the histogram’s bin boundaries.

The bootstrap distribution describes how the estimated mean varies when we resample the observed data. We use it to approximate how the sample mean would vary if we could repeatedly collect new samples from the population. In the percentile-bootstrap method, we take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these bootstrap means and use those same endpoints as an approximate 95% confidence interval for the population mean. The orange region below shows the percentile calculation; the orange bar above shows the resulting confidence interval, with matching numerical endpoints. The interval contains approximately 95% of the bootstrap means, not 95% of the observed cars; its intended confidence coverage depends on the bootstrap approximation being appropriate.

The first panel combines the observed values with the same interval as a horizontal error bar and a larger dot at the original sample mean. All three panels have the same width and aligned horsepower axes. The dot stays fixed as resamples accumulate; the interval endpoints update. This is the usual point-estimate-and-interval summary, not a point at the current resample’s mean or at the midpoint of the interval. The dot remains visible when the interval is switched off. Inside the histogram, orange text identifies the middle 95% and its percentile boundaries; in the observed-data panel, it identifies the confidence interval for the population mean. Gray text identifies the original sample mean, and black text identifies the current resample’s mean, matching the respective marks. The interval is available from the first resample, but its endpoints are unstable when based on very few means. With just one resample, both percentiles equal that resample’s mean, so the interval has zero width; this does not indicate certainty about the population mean. Continue accumulating resamples to see the calculation stabilize.

Notice that every resample still contains 20 observations, even when the histogram contains 1,000 means. Increasing the number of resamples makes the bootstrap calculation more stable; it does not add new information to the original sample. The slider reveals a fixed sequence of resamples generated with a random seed, so returning to the same position gives the same result.

Exercise

Why can a car appear several times in one resample? Does the orange interval contain 95% of the individual cars’ horsepower values?

Solution

Sampling with replacement makes every observed car available for every draw, including cars already selected in that resample. The orange interval summarizes uncertainty in the mean, using percentiles of the bootstrap means; it is not an interval for individual cars. We call it a 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval because it aims for 95% repeated-sampling coverage of the population mean. Actual coverage depends on the sampling assumptions and how well the observed sample represents the population; taking these percentiles does not guarantee exact 95% coverage.

Interval endpoints are not walls

  • Nor should we assume that every kind of error bar must exclude observations: a minimum-to-maximum interval includes the observed range by construction. The interpretation depends on what the interval actually represents.

Readers can interpret the endpoints of an error bar as the limits of what is possible. This is sometimes called a deterministic construal error: treating an uncertainty display as a hard constraint. An interval summarizing part of a distribution need not contain all its possible values. For a confidence interval, remember that the coverage statement concerns repeated sampling; a particular interval can miss the population quantity.

Nor should we assume that every kind of error bar must exclude observations: a minimum-to-maximum interval includes the observed range by construction. The interpretation depends on what the interval actually represents.

Showing alternative outcomes

A single line and shaded band can make one fitted curve seem inevitable, with uncertainty appearing only as a little movement up or down. Showing alternative curves makes the possible variation in shape more explicit.

A hypothetical outcome plot (HOP) cycles through sampled possibilities, rather than drawing them all at once. In this example, each frame shows an alternative fitted curve from the model’s posterior distribution. The animation shows uncertainty in the fitted relationship, not a time sequence in which the cars change.

Hypothetical outcome plots

The static comparison above remains useful for print and for readers who prefer to inspect several alternatives at once. For an animation, outcomes should be sampled to represent the modeled uncertainty, rather than selected to exaggerate stability or variation. Rare but important outcomes may need explicit annotation or a separate view, since a short animation can easily omit them.

Solution

Position uncertainty, not storm size

  • Its widening instead represents increasing uncertainty about the future position of the storm’s center.

  • Its boundary is not a line separating dangerous from safe locations: the center can move outside the cone, and hazardous weather can extend beyond it.

  • A collection of different models is not automatically a set of equally probable outcomes.

A forecast cone can be mistaken for the storm’s physical size. Its widening instead represents increasing uncertainty about the future position of the storm’s center. Its boundary is not a line separating dangerous from safe locations: the center can move outside the cone, and hazardous weather can extend beyond it.

Alternative tracks make the uncertainty about location more explicit, but viewers still need to know what the tracks represent and how they were generated. A collection of different models is not automatically a set of equally probable outcomes. For decisions such as evacuation, pair the uncertainty display with information about the relevant hazards and guidance, rather than asking readers to infer risk from the center line or cone alone.

The same general lesson applies to statistical bands: explain what is uncertain, what the displayed region means, and which conclusions its boundaries do and do not support. We now turn to constructing intervals with the plotting libraries.

To summarize the uncertainty in the mean horsepower for each origin and year, we can draw a confidence band. At each year, the lower and upper edges show the endpoints of an interval; shading connects those intervals across years.

Altair

mark_errorband() calculates and draws the interval. Setting extent='ci' requests a 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the mean, calculated separately for each combination of year and origin. The default extent is 'stderr', which instead shows the mean plus or minus one standard error. Use 'stdev' to show one standard deviation. These choices have different meanings, so specify the extent explicitly.

ggplot

In ggplot, we can create confidence bands with geom_ribbon(). Previously we passed summary functions such as mean to fun. Here we use fun.data, because a ribbon needs both a lower and an upper bound.

The mean_cl_boot function returns three values: y, ymin, and ymax. These are the mean and the lower and upper endpoints of its bootstrap confidence interval. The ribbon uses the two endpoints. This function requires the Hmisc package, which is included in the textbook’s R environment.

color = NA removes the ribbon’s boundary lines, leaving only the shaded area. The random seed makes the R bootstrap reproducible. Both libraries use bootstrap intervals, but their endpoints can differ slightly because the resamples are random and are generated separately.

We would need to calculate the fit and interval endpoints with a statistical library first, then draw the line and shaded area in Altair. For example, scikit-learn fits could be bootstrapped by repeatedly resampling the observations and refitting the model, but scikit-learn does not supply these confidence bands automatically. We use ggplot for the compact examples here.

Adding mark_errorband(extent='ci') to the raw data would instead calculate separate bootstrap intervals at each acceleration time; those would not be confidence intervals for the fitted regression or LOESS curve.

ggplot

For linear regression, geom_smooth(method = 'lm') can display both the fitted line and a confidence band. We explicitly set se = TRUE and level = 0.95, although these are the defaults. Despite the argument’s name, se = TRUE enables a confidence band; it does not request a band of plus or minus one standard error. Mapping both color and fill to origin gives each line and its band matching colors.

The shaded region is a pointwise 95% confidence interval for the fitted mean horsepower. It is not a standard-deviation band or a prediction interval for individual cars. These intervals use the linear model’s estimated standard errors and a t-distribution, rather than the groupwise bootstrap calculation used earlier.

We can also show a confidence band around a LOESS curve. Here we use R’s local quadratic, non-robust fit and a span of 0.75, making those defaults explicit.

LOESS with a confidence band

trend_points + geom_smooth(method = 'loess')

ggplot

For LOESS, ggplot uses an approximate pointwise confidence interval based on the smoother’s estimated standard errors and a t-based approximation. The curve and band depend on the chosen span and fitting settings; the band does not account for uncertainty about which smoothing settings to choose.

In both charts, the bands describe uncertainty in the fitted mean relationship, not the spread of the underlying observations. Keeping the points visible helps distinguish those two quantities. As with the annual-mean intervals, pointwise 95% coverage does not imply 95% simultaneous coverage of the entire curve.
