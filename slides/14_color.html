Color

Learning outcomes

  1. Describe hue, saturation, and lightness.
  2. Match the palette type to the meaning of the data. Choose categorical, sequential, diverging, or cyclic palettes for unordered groups, numerical magnitude, departures from a reference, or values that wrap around.
  3. Explain perceptual uniformity and the role of lightness. Recognize how uneven or reversing lightness can obscure numerical order and create artificial boundaries.
  4. Set color mappings, domains, and directions deliberately. Use meaningful diverging midpoints, symmetric limits when comparing equal departures, and consistent category-to-color mappings across related layers and views.
  5. Highlight selected observations without losing context. Use restrained colors and familiar associations to guide attention rather than assigning every observation a competing color.
  6. Choose annotations, direct labels, or ordered legends. Reduce lookup effort and clutter while keeping values and series identities clear.

Use a cyclic palette when the underlying variable wraps around, such as an angle or a position within a yearly cycle. Similar endpoint colors communicate that connection, but would be misleading for unrelated low and high endpoints on a linear scale.

Guessing a numerical sequence from hue

Use a diverging palette when departures above and below a meaningful reference have different interpretations. Examples include change from a baseline or correlations centered on zero; the middle should not be chosen merely because it is halfway between the observed extremes.

A categorical palette should make groups distinguishable without implying a numerical order. Its usefulness still depends on the number of categories, the background, and readers’ color vision.

Regardless of which palette we choose, coloring every country can create decoration rather than useful identification. Highlight selected comparisons, divide the display into smaller views, or use direct labels when that makes the intended question easier to answer.

When colors have a strong existing association, working with it can reduce the reader’s effort. The bar values are unchanged, but using blue for water and green for land makes the categories easier to anticipate.
