5  Groups

NoteLearning outcomes & Chapter summary
  1. Explain the two main purposes of grouping:
    • Making direct comparisons between groups, e.g., observations in group A tend to have higher values than those in group B.
    • Avoiding incorrect generalizations, e.g., there could be an overall positive trend when all observations are viewed together, while the data for individual groups displays negative trends or no trend at all.
  2. Evaluate which grouping strategy is the most suitable in a specific context:
    • Color (optionally combined with shape) distinguishes groups while keeping observations close together on shared positional scales for comparing values.
    • Grouping by position gives each category its own lane for comparing values of another variable.
    • Faceting is often effective when comparing the pattern or trend of a group to those of other groups since it shows the observations for each group separately.
  3. Apply grouping strategies using color, shape, and position (including facets) to visually distinguish between categories

5.1 Why bother?

So far, you have created declarative visualizations by representing observations as point marks on a canvas, using position and color to represent quantitative variables. This is the foundation for all the visualizations we will make, but it is rarely sufficient on its own to give a complete picture of the data. In particular, real-world datasets might show overall trends that disappear or change when individual groups are explored more closely. The goal of grouping is to distinguish between groups by basing the visual appearance or placement of points in a chart on the value of a categorical variable. Let’s see this in action in the following exercise.

This gives us a possible explanation for the surprising overall trend. Income level could be a confounding variable, influencing both alcohol consumption and life expectancy. The chart suggests this possibility but does not establish those causal effects. Crucially, the points represent countries, not people: we cannot conclude that the people who drink heavily are the same people who live longer. We would need individual-level data and an appropriate study design to investigate that relationship.

The data hasn’t changed, but our view of it has. Grouping turns a puzzling overall trend into a more granular question: does the relationship hold within comparable groups?

5.3 The two main purposes of grouping

As we have seen in the exercises above, grouping can have major benefits. We can divide these benefits into two main categories, both of which help us arrive at more accurate conclusions about our data:

  1. Directly compare observations between groups.
  2. Reveal patterns within individual groups that are obscured when viewing the data from all groups together.

These benefits are often connected and can be explored simultaneously. When we grouped the alcohol consumption data by income level, the positive relationship in the pooled data was much less apparent within groups. Grouping also made it easier to compare observations between groups.

Simpson’s paradox

Differences between overall and within-group relationships are central to Simpson’s paradox (named after the statistician Edward H. Simpson, rather than the TV series). In the classic form of this phenomenon, a trend within individual groups reverses when the groups are combined. Grouping can also reveal trends that are weak or absent in the pooled data.

Below we show a simplified illustration of this aggregation effect. Notice how the overall dataset shows little apparent relationship between X and Y, but when the observations are colored by group, you can see that both groups exhibit a negative trend between X and Y: larger values of X tend to be associated with smaller values of Y, and vice versa. This is a prime example of the utility of grouping in deepening our understanding of a dataset; without it, we would have missed the within-group relationships.

5.4 What type of column can we use for grouping?

In general, we group data by “categorical” columns. If there is a natural order to the categories (e.g., education levels or letter grades), we can be more precise and call the column “ordinal”. If there is no such order, we say that the column is “nominal” (e.g., types of fruit or color names). As a general rule, we don’t group data by continuous variables or discrete variable with many values, but we could if we first turn them into interval categories e.g. converting age in years into the intervals 18–24, 25–34, and 35–44. In general, we want relatively few unique values when we group by a variable so that the visualization is easy to digest, but there are exceptions, particularly when creating faceted plots as we will see later.

5.5 Channels for grouping

Generally speaking, position and color are the two most effective visual channels to use for grouping. Shape can also be used, but often either in combination with one of the other two or for a specific purpose.

Let’s take a look at a familiar example: the traffic light. Do you think that traffic lights are well designed? At first glance, their use of red and green presents a challenge for people with red–green color vision deficiency. Thankfully, signals can communicate their meaning through position and shape as well as color, as in the pedestrian signals below.

Shape up

Some regions also use shape to distinguish traffic signals for cars, as in this example from Nova Scotia in eastern Canada.

Using several visual channels for the same distinction gives viewers more than one cue to interpret. Even traffic signals for cars that lack distinct shapes typically use a consistent arrangement, so drivers can use position as well as color to distinguish the signals.

Color

The human brain can detect color differences with little cognitive effort. Color is a preattentive feature3 which means that the brain can tell apart colors even without conscious attention. This is heightened when there is a high contrast, such as between the complementary colors blue and orange.

This is great news for grouping! By encoding our categorical variable as color, we can leverage the brain’s ability to infer groups. Conveniently, color doesn’t alter the layout or structure of the data visualization, so it’s an efficient way to compare the exact values of data points between groups.

In the second exercise of this chapter, we already saw how color could be used to group our life expectancy data by income group. Let’s now see what the code to create this chart looks like:

  • Altair
  • ggplot

Here we use color='income_group':

import altair as alt
import pandas as pd

income_df = pd.read_csv('data/income_lifeexp_alcohol.csv')

alt.Chart(income_df).mark_point().encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='life_expectancy_at_65',
    color='income_group',
)

Here we use color = income_group:

library(tidyverse)

WarningAvoid using color for more than 4-6 groups

Using too many colors or colors that are too similar can blur the differences between groups. There are also several other important aspects of using colors effectively, such as how to ensure accessibility, particularly for people with color vision deficiencies. We will learn more about these topics in a later chapter, but for now it’s enough to remember that when there are more than about 4–6 groups in the data, it might be better to break it down into separate charts instead of grouping by color. Shape

Shape is a less effective grouping strategy on its own, but it can be combined with color to provide an additional visual cue.

The syntax for mapping a variable to the shape channel is similar to that for color:

  • Altair
  • ggplot
alt.Chart(income_df).mark_point().encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='life_expectancy_at_65',
    color='income_group',
    shape='income_group'
)

Setting filled=True fills all the points with a solid color, making the shapes easier to distinguish:

alt.Chart(income_df).mark_point(filled=True).encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='life_expectancy_at_65',
    color='income_group',
    shape='income_group'
)
ggplot(income_df) +
    aes(
        x = percentage_heavy_drinkers,
        y = life_expectancy_at_65,
        color = income_group,
        shape = income_group
    ) +
    geom_point()

While this combined approach can make grouping clearer, there are some pitfalls. For instance, hollow symbols limit the total area that can be colored, reducing the impact of color. Filled shapes can make color easier to see when combining shape and color. Additionally, smaller shapes are difficult to distinguish, so when plotting a dense scatterplot of small marks, try restricting the number of unique shapes to three or fewer, or consider avoiding using shape altogether.

WarningAvoid using color and shape for different variables

It is often difficult to interpret charts that map one variable to color and another to shape. This tends to lead to too much visual information and it is difficult to distinguish the many different combinations visually. The exception is if the chart is relatively simple with few observations and few combinations of the shape and color variable; then this combination might work.

The many combinations of income group and region in this chart are hard to separate visually, which makes this chart ineffective:

  • ggplot
alt.Chart(income_df).mark_point(filled=True).encode(

WarningAvoid using size to group by a categorical variable

Size is often used to represent the magnitude of quantitative data. Under limited circumstances, it can also be used for grouping data according to a categorical variable, but in general this is best avoided.

Since size has an inherent direction from small to large, it works better for ordinal than nominal categorical data. One example is the World Bank income levels, ranging from low to high. However, even when used for ordinal data, it is often difficult to tell different sizes apart, as you can see in the chart below.

Position

X and Y position

Grouping by position often creates the clearest separation between groups. Distributing the groups along the x- or y-axis gives each group its own visual lane, preventing observations from different groups from overlapping. This means that there is no risk for overlap between groups and we can generally visualize many more groups when we separate them by position rather than color or shape. However, the grouping column now occupies one positional visual channel, so there is only one positional channel left for encoding the values we want to compare between the groups.

Below, we distribute the regions along the y-axis. This leaves us with a choice of whether to use the x-axis for percentage_heavy_drinkers or life_expectancy_at_65. We cannot represent both quantitative variables with position in this chart.

  • Altair
  • ggplot
alt.Chart(income_df).mark_point().encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='region',
)
ggplot(income_df) +
    aes(
        x = percentage_heavy_drinkers,
        y = region,
    ) +
    geom_point()
WarningAvoid rotated category labels along the x-axis

We could swap the x and y variables above, but then the region labels would be rotated and harder to read. Short category labels that fit horizontally can work well on the x-axis. For longer labels, placing the categories on the y-axis usually makes the text easier to read.

Faceting

Faceting takes the concept of grouping to the next level. Instead of using different visual channels within a single chart, generates multiple charts (or “facets”), each dedicated to a grouped subset of the data. Faceting is particularly effective for highlighting distinct trends and patterns within each subset, as well as identifying extreme values or outliers within groups. We could think of faceting as a type of position grouping, but its advantage is that it keeps both the x- and y-axes free for other variables. Facetting generally supports comparison of many groups, just like grouping by x or y position.

Let’s create a separate facet for each income group, so that the relationship between percentage_heavy_drinkers and life_expectancy_at_65 within each group stands out. We can lay out the facets differently depending on what we are most interested in comparing. If the y-axis is the primary channel for comparison, we would spread the facets out horizontally in a single row. If the x-axis is the primary channel for comparison, we would spread the facets out vertically in a single column. In either case, the scales should be shared for direct comparisons. Neither axis is our sole focus here: we are interested in comparing the overall pattern within each group, so we will lay out the facets in a compact grid to save space.

  • Altair
  • ggplot

To facet by income group, we can append .facet('income_group') to our chart. We create a 2 × 2 grid of facets by using the columns parameter.

alt.Chart(income_df, height=150, width=150).mark_point().encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='life_expectancy_at_65',
).facet(
    'income_group',
    columns=2
)

In addition to faceting on a single variable in the data, faceting can also be done over two variables: one spread horizontally and one spread out vertically in the faceted charts. In Altair, we would use the column and row parameters to achieve this. For example, to have facets that are grouped by both income group and region, we could use facet(column='income_group', row='region'). This can be effective when we need to group our data further, but it can make the chart difficult to take in at a glance, so it is best used when there are fewer categories in the faceting variables.

To facet by income group, we can add + facet_wrap(vars(income_group)) to our chart. The vars() function specifies which columns to use for faceting, just as aes() specifies the mappings from variables to visual channels.

By default, facet_wrap() arranges these four groups in a 2 × 2 grid, which is appropriate for our visualization. Here, we set the ncol argument explicitly to illustrate how you can control the number of columns in the facet grid.

ggplot(income_df) +
    aes(
        x = percentage_heavy_drinkers,
        y = life_expectancy_at_65
    ) +
    geom_point() +
    facet_wrap(vars(income_group), ncol = 2)

In addition to faceting on a single variable in the data, faceting can also be done over two variables: one spread horizontally and one spread out vertically in the faceted charts. In ggplot, we would replace facet_wrap() with facet_grid() to do this. For example, to have facets that are grouped by both income group and region, we could use facet_grid(rows = vars(region), cols = vars(income_group)). This can be effective when we need to group our data further, but it can make the chart difficult to take in at a glance, so it is best used when there are fewer categories in the faceting variables.

As you can see above, faceting makes it easier to observe the overall pattern for each income group and how tightly clustered the values are. It also reveals potential outliers in the upper-middle-income facet: we can easily spot two countries with a higher percentage of heavy drinkers than the others in that group. On the other hand, it is now harder to compare individual values between groups because the points are spread across different charts instead of being close together in a single chart.

5.6 Grouping within facets

All the previous grouping strategies can be used within each facet as additional layers of grouping. This nested grouping can be useful for dividing our data according to multiple categorical variables, but it can become confusing if we try to fit too much into a single visualization. How many grouping variables you can use depends on how well separated the groups are and how many observations you have. More observations and less separation between groups mean that you need to be more careful and may need fewer layers of grouping to keep the visualization easy to interpret.

Let’s try out an example of how we can use faceting together with other grouping strategies.

Let’s say you are primarily interested in the relationship between life expectancy and heavy drinking across different regions. As a secondary comparison, you would also like to see how different income groups vary within the same region. Which visual channels would you use to group the chart?

Ex-hintHint

You could e.g. use faceting and coloring, but which one should you use for which grouping variable?

Ex-solutionSolution

Since we are interesting in comparing the relationship between two variables, faceting is a suitable choice as it gives two free positional channels (x and y) to map the two variables to for each group. Since we are primarily interested in how this relationship changes between regions, we will create one facet per region. Then we use another visual channel, such as color (or color and shape) to highlight the income groups within each region.

  • ggplot
alt.Chart(income_df, height=200, width=200).mark_point().encode(
    x='percentage_heavy_drinkers',
    y='life_expectancy_at_65',
    color='income_group'
).facet(
    'region',
    columns=2
)
ggplot(income_df) +
    aes(
        x = percentage_heavy_drinkers,
        y = life_expectancy_at_65,
        color = income_group
    ) +
    geom_point() +
    facet_wrap(vars(region), ncol = 2)
